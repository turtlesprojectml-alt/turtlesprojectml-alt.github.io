Abstract

Machine-learning surrogates are increasingly used to accelerate computational fluid dynamics, yet progress is limited by the lack of benchmarks capturing realistic, time-dependent turbulent flows. We introduce TurtLES, a 14 TB dataset of 3D turbulent cylinder wakes in the shear-layer transition regime, computed via high-fidelity implicit large-eddy simulations. The dataset contains 400 trajectories of 400 time steps each, with 3–11 million points per frame, spanning variations in geometry, Reynolds number, and angle of attack. Unlike existing datasets, TurtLES combines (i) physically realistic turbulence with an active energy cascade driven by vortex stretching, (ii) dense irregular meshes, and (iii) long-horizon temporal dynamics. We benchmark state-of-the-art neural operators on autoregressive rollouts and identify key failure modes, including temporal instability, loss of small-scale energy, and challenges in learning on large irregular domains. TurtLES provides a new benchmark for evaluating surrogate models on 3D turbulent wakes at industrially relevant mesh resolution.

Contributions

  1. Dense irregular 3D data. Dynamic three-dimensional flow on unstructured meshes with millions of points per frame, beyond the resolution of regular-grid or coarse point-cloud benchmarks.
  2. Long-horizon evaluation. Hundreds of autoregressive time steps in chaotic, geometry-dependent wakes.
  3. Physically grounded assessment. Turbulent surrogates are scored on high-frequency quantities such as fluctuation statistics, spanwise energy spectra, and per-case wake-probe spectra, in addition to field-wise errors.

Dataset Overview

TurtLES is constructed within a four-dimensional parameter space. Geometry is parameterised through the super-ellipse (Lamé curve) |x/a|n + |y/b|n = 1, with the exponent n ∈ {1, 2, 5, ∞} selecting diamond, elliptical, rounded-rectangle and rectangular shapes. The half-thickness is fixed at b = 1/2, giving a constant object thickness D = 2b = 1, while the semi-axis a is sampled log-uniformly in [1/6, 3/2], so that aspect ratios span 1/3 to 3. The inflow is set by the Reynolds number Re ∈ [1000, 3900], based on D, and the angle of attack α ∈ [0°, 45°]. In this range the wake transitions to turbulence while the boundary layer remains laminar. A Sobol’ sequence draws Ns = 400 samples that evenly cover the hypercube.

Each case is solved with a high-order Flux Reconstruction implicit LES scheme implemented in PyFR, on a Gmsh-generated mesh whose 2D xy-plane is extruded into 30 structured span-wise layers. Each element holds 75–125 solution points, giving 2.9 M to 10.5 M unique points per case within the stored region. The numerical setup is validated against DNS and experimental data for four benchmark cases: a circular cylinder at Re = 1000 and 3900, a square cylinder at Re = 2000, and a 5:1 rectangle at Re = 3000.

Data Structure

Data collection starts only once the initial numerical transient has been washed out. Each case then stores a single set of node coordinates plus 400 snapshots of the pressure p, density ρ, and velocity components (u, v, w), saved in HDF5 as one coordinate file and four temporal chunks of 100 frames. Snapshots are restricted to nodes inside the box x/D ∈ [−3, 10], y/D ∈ [−3.5, 3.5], z/D ∈ [0, 2π]. The sampling interval Δts = 0.04732 (in units of D/U) retains the inertial range of the turbulence spectrum; sub-sampling acts in time only, so every snapshot keeps the full spatial resolution.

Each case directory also contains power spectra of the streamwise (u) and cross-stream (v) velocity at three centreline probes placed 2.5D, 3.5D and 4.5D behind the object, together with the script used to compute them. These give per-case evidence of the turbulent cascade and a physically grounded target for evaluating surrogates.

Flow Visualisations

For each shape family, one case is shown at low (Re ≤ 1300) and one at high Reynolds number (Re > 3000). At low Re the wake consists of fewer, thicker and more coherent vortex tubes, whereas at high Re it breaks up into a much denser field of fine-scale structures.

Splits and mini-TurtLES

Trajectories are split into fixed training, validation and test sets of 320 / 40 / 40 cases, released with the dataset. Each shape family holds 100 trajectories and is split exactly 80/10/10, and within each family the assignment is optimised so that the distribution of (Re, α, log a) in every subset matches that of the full dataset. This yields an in-distribution interpolation split; extrapolation splits (for example, holding out a whole shape family or all cases above a Reynolds-number threshold) are straightforward to build from the released data. A circular-cylinder reference case (Re = 1000, a = 0.5, α = 0°, n = 2) is distributed separately.

To lower the barrier to entry, mini-TurtLES defines standardised subsets that shrink the dataset along two independent axes. Geometry tiers (G4–G1) drop shape families while keeping the full range of Re, α and a; temporal tiers (T100–T400) keep the first 100–400 snapshots of each trajectory. Tiers combine freely, for example G2-T200. A 5 GB TurtLES-debug subset, holding the first 100 snapshots of the reference circular-cylinder case, is provided for installation checks and dataloader validation. All subsets are described in the miniturtles.md file shipped with the dataset.

Benchmarks

We evaluate four neural-operator baselines, UPT, GINO, Transolver++, and CALM-PDE (with the scaling improvements of ECHO), on next-frame prediction of p, ρ, u, v and w. All models are trained on the 320 training trajectories to predict the residual between consecutive frames, conditioned on Re, under a shared budget of 100k steps (about 20 h on one H100). At test time each model rolls out the full 399-step trajectory from the initial condition alone. We report the relative L2 error, the variance-scaled RMSE (VRMSE, where predicting the per-frame spatial mean scores 1), the RMS log-power error of spanwise energy spectra in low-, mid- and high-wavenumber bands, and the GPU time to predict one full trajectory.

The results expose four complementary failure modes:

  • Short-horizon error. Under the shared budget, CALM-PDE and UPT reach the best next-frame accuracy, while Transolver++ and GINO score a VRMSE at or above 1, no better than predicting the per-frame spatial mean.
  • Long-horizon instability. CALM-PDE stays stable over the full rollout (VRMSE 0.46 → 0.79), whereas UPT drifts to 5.1 and Transolver++ and GINO exceed 30 in the final window.
  • Loss of small-scale fidelity. CALM-PDE tracks the reference spectrum closely, UPT shows larger high-wavenumber errors, and Transolver++ strongly over-predicts energy at intermediate and high wavenumbers. A persistence baseline that repeats the initial state also matches the time-averaged spectrum, so spectral agreement must be read together with field-level and rollout errors.
  • Limited acceleration. Every surrogate is faster than the solver in GPU-seconds, but only by about 3× (GINO) to 16× (UPT), a modest margin compared with speedups commonly reported on smaller problems.

Limitations

TurtLES covers one physical regime: turbulent cylinder wakes in the shear-layer transition range, and whether the observed failure modes transfer to other geometries or Reynolds regimes remains open. The reference trajectories come from implicit LES, so surrogate errors are measured against this solver rather than against DNS or experiment. Snapshots are sub-sampled in time to keep storage tractable, and the resulting window is too short for converged averaged statistics such as Reynolds stresses. The four baselines are a starting point, not an exhaustive account of how neural operators perform on this class of flows.
